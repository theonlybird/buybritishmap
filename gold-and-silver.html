Gold & Silver

Almost anything can be called British if you only look at the last step. A shirt sewn in Kent from Chinese cloth, a mug decorated in Stoke on a blank made in Asia, a ring set in Hatton Garden around an imported casting — each is honestly “made in Britain” in some sense, and each keeps a very different amount of money in the country. The further back a supply chain runs in Britain, the more of your money stays here. A coat sewn in a British factory supports that factory. The same coat cut from British-woven cloth also supports a mill, and the mill supports a spinner, and the spinner supports a farm. Those upstream trades — the mills, spinners, tanneries, clay houses and foundries — are the parts that are hardest to rebuild once they close, because the skills and the machinery go together. That is what the two badges are for: not to sort good businesses from bad, but to show you how much of the chain is still here.

Silver

Genuinely made in the UK, from a main material that is imported or whose origin isn’t published.

Silver is not a consolation prize. These are real workshops and factories employing real people, and for several trades Silver is simply the best that Britain can currently offer — the upstream industry no longer exists here at any scale. A Northampton shoemaker using European calf is doing highly skilled work that supports a town; there is no British tannery producing that leather to buy from. A great many Silver listings are also only Silver because a business hasn’t said who spins its yarn or rolls its steel. One clear answer would move them up. Buying Silver is buying British.

Gold

Fully made or grown in the UK — including the main material.

Gold means the chain runs deeper: the cloth was woven here, the clay was dug or prepared here, the metal was worked from bullion at a British bench, the food was grown on the farm. Raw inputs that Britain simply cannot produce — cotton, cashmere fibre, gold bullion, gemstones — don’t count against a business, because no amount of effort would change them. What counts is whether the first properly manufactured stage happened in Britain. We keep Gold deliberately strict and rule-based rather than a matter of judgement, because the moment it becomes discretionary it stops meaning anything.

What qualifies, category by category

Category Gold Silver
Farm shops Effectively everything sold is British — grown or reared on the farm itself, or bought in from other British growers and producers. No imported produce lines. A farm shop selling its own meat and veg alongside other British suppliers. Mostly British, and often growing a great deal of it, but stocking a meaningful amount of imported food alongside — the large organic farm shop model. Daylesford and Riverford sit here: excellent British farms with imported lines on the shelves.
Clothing The garment is made here and the main fabric was milled, woven, knitted or tanned in the UK. Raw fibre is always imported and doesn’t count against it. For knitwear the yarn must be evidenced as UK-spun — knitting a jumper here creates the fabric, but the spinner still has to be named. Cloth woven at a British mill, or knitwear on named British-spun yarn. Genuinely cut, sewn, knitted or made in a UK workshop, but the main fabric or leather is imported or its origin isn’t published. Northampton shoemaking is the standing precedent — superb British construction, imported calf leather.
Ceramics Formed, fired and decorated in the UK, and the clay body itself is British — dug here, or prepared here by a British clay house, or mixed in-house. Imported glaze chemicals and stains don’t count against it, in the same way dyes don’t for cloth. Cornish china clay or Devon ball clay, worked in a British pottery. Made in a British pottery, but from a ready-made imported body, or the clay’s origin can’t be evidenced. Decorating imported blanks isn’t Silver — it doesn’t qualify for the map at all.
Jewellery & Watches Jewellery fabricated in the UK from grain or bullion, at a British bench or casting house. Bullion counts as a raw material, and imported gemstones are fine. For watches, a movement that is substantially UK-made. UK-mined materials — Welsh gold, Whitby jet, Blue John — are noted in the listing. British bench work — setting, finishing, assembly — on imported castings, mounts or chains. For watches, genuine UK making and assembly around an imported movement. A British hallmark is not evidence of British making: import hallmarks exist.
Cutlery & Cookware Forged, ground, spun or cut in the UK and the principal material is British — British steel or iron, or British-quarried stone. Rare, because Britain no longer has a stainless strip industry serving this trade. Genuinely made in a British workshop or factory, but the steel or iron stock is imported or its supplier isn’t disclosed. Nearly the whole trade sits here, including long-established Sheffield firms. Real forging and finishing in Sheffield on steel that came from elsewhere.

Two rules that sit above all of the above. Where the origin of a main material isn’t clearly evidenced, a business gets Silver until it’s confirmed — we never give the benefit of the doubt for Gold. And badges can never be bought: no business pays to be listed, promoted or upgraded, and only evidence moves anyone up.

If we’ve got something wrong

Every badge on this map is researched by hand from what businesses publish about themselves, and we try hard to get it right. But supply chains change, websites go out of date, and some of these judgements turn on a single line buried in a product description. We will get some of them wrong. If you think a business has the wrong badge — whether you run it, buy from it, or simply know the trade better than we do — please tell us and point us at the evidence, and we’ll review it and correct it.
